# Positive craft — what to aim FOR, not just what to remove

Every other reference file in this skill is a negative-pattern catalog:
what to cut, what to flag, what to strip. This file is the complementary
positive target — properties of genuinely good, human prose, derived from
close reading of real published essayistic writing (source: five named
Paul Graham essays). Explicitly **not** "write like Graham" — his
particular mannerisms are excluded on purpose — these are craft properties
his writing happens to make legible, portable to any voice.

## Eleven properties

1. **Slack sentences are allowed to exist.** A sentence written at
   half-pressure ("We'll see." "Or not, I don't know.") is not filler —
   it's the audible sound of a person thinking rather than a text being
   assembled. One or two per piece. This is a direct antidote to
   over-punched "clean slop," where every single sentence is maximally
   forceful and nothing ever just trails off the way real thought does.
2. **Uncertainty goes where the uncertainty actually is.** A hedge must
   mark the *exact* claim it applies to, precisely — not smeared as
   blanket insurance over the whole paragraph (AI hedging), and not
   absent entirely (AI overconfidence). The check: is confidence uneven
   across the text, matching where the author's actual knowledge is
   uneven? Flat confidence throughout — whether flatly high or flatly
   hedged — both fail this check.
3. **One homely analogy per point, maximum.** Never stack two analogies
   for the same idea. Never build an analogy out of another abstraction
   ("a paradigm functioning as a framework" is not an analogy, it's two
   abstractions glued together). A working analogy is physical or
   domestic and does real explanatory work, not decorative work.
4. **Endings without ceremony.** None of the reference essays end on a
   recap, a stated lesson, or an aphorism. The last sentence should be
   the last actual thing there is to say — not a bow tied on the package.
   Direct positive-framing counterpart to this skill's existing rule
   against conclusory tropes (`sentence-patterns.md` §13).
5. **Concreteness has a texture, not just a presence.** "Use specifics"
   isn't enough — the specifics have to be a *particular kind*: physical,
   countable, slightly odd, remembered rather than invented ("$3,000 a
   month," "rolly bags at Tuesday dinners"). A detail that's too smooth to
   be real ("the team gathered around a whiteboard") itself reads as
   generated, even though it's technically "a specific." This gives a
   test for what makes a specific *convincing* versus still-generic,
   which the word-blacklist files alone don't define.
6. **A genuine surprise or turn, not a signposted one.** The strongest
   moments in good essay writing arrive without an announcement ("Here's
   the interesting part:") — they just happen, mid-sentence sometimes.
7. **Digression that pays off, or is honestly abandoned.** A tangent that
   circles back deliberately, or one that's dropped without pretending it
   was always going somewhere — both read as human. A tangent forced into
   relevance with a clumsy transition reads as machine-generated damage
   control.
8. **A stated opinion that could be wrong.** Not hedged into
   unfalsifiability — a real claim, stated plainly, that the reader could
   in principle disagree with and say so.
9. **Rhythm that follows thought, not a template.** Sentence length and
   paragraph shape driven by what the idea actually needs at that moment,
   not by a pre-set cadence (short-punchy-open, build, long-explain,
   short-punchy-close).
10. **A detail the writer clearly didn't need to include.** Something
    slightly beside the point that survives anyway because it's true and
    the writer liked it — the opposite of AI's relentless
    on-topic-ness.
11. **Register drift within bounds** — a paragraph can get a little more
    casual or a little more precise than its neighbors without breaking
    the piece, the way a person's actual voice varies moment to moment;
    perfectly uniform register throughout a long piece is itself a tell.

Sources: asavvin-pixel/unslop, harvested GitHub skill, distilled and
re-expressed — no verbatim text copied. Use alongside the negative-pattern
files (`word-blacklist-en.md`, `word-blacklist-ru.md`,
`sentence-patterns.md`) — removing tells gets you to neutral; these
properties are what gets a rewrite past neutral into genuinely good.
